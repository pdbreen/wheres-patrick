Naples

Link to this day

Naples. A bit of an odd schedule today: the ship arrives at 7am, but the meeting time for the Herculaneum tour on the ship is 1pm. So I spent the morning wandering Naples and café hopping for cappuccino until I could get lunch. I started in Galleria Umberto because the building is so impressive. The wandering after that brought me to the Church of the New Jesus. The exterior was very plain — I almost didn't realize it was anything special — but I saw the open door and went in. It definitely exceeded the expectations set by the exterior; it was quite ornate inside. Lunch was street pizza — it was before noon, so not a lot of places were serving yet. The place I chose was meh.

The Herculaneum tour was pretty good. It was buried by the same eruption of Vesuvius that destroyed Pompeii in AD 79, but where Pompeii went under falling ash, Herculaneum was hit by superheated surges that carbonized its wood — which is why so much of it survives. Very different than Pompeii. The excavation site is extremely small comparatively, but some of the frescoes and mosaics were in pretty good shape. Because of how it was buried, they also had a lot of preserved carbonized wood furniture and some wood structures like ceilings, and a boat in the museum. The skeletons were replicas — but they did find many in the boathouses, like they're shown.

MP Art Duo is the farewell show. I'm assuming it will differ from the welcome aboard show they did. Daylight savings ends tonight here, so an extra hour of sleep before doing the immigration processing for the transatlantic in the morning.

Herculaneum, up close. The town was smaller and wealthier than Pompeii — a seaside resort for well-off Romans — and the eruption caught it differently: the first pyroclastic surge arrived in the night with heat so intense that it killed instantly the hundreds sheltering in the waterfront boathouses, where the remains of some 300 people were found in the 1980s. Buried under roughly 20 metres of volcanic material, the town stayed sealed until workmen digging a well struck its theatre in 1709. Much of it is still unexcavated beneath the modern town of Ercolano — including the Villa of the Papyri, whose charred scrolls are the only library to survive from antiquity. The Roman boat in the museum was found on the ancient shoreline in 1982, preserved by the same mud that buried everything else. Archaeological Park of Herculaneum

Delos and the windmills. Back in Mykonos Town, the Kato Mili windmills in his photos were built by the Venetians in the 16th century to mill the island’s grain; most had stopped working by the mid-20th century, and they have been the island’s postcard ever since.

Santorini

Link to this day

Santorini. No line for the tender at about 9am, and only a bit of a wait for the cable car — still better than walking up. The island is the rim of a drowned volcano — its cliff towns perch above a caldera flooded by one of the largest eruptions of the ancient world. I started with a wander through Fira before the longer walk to Imerovigli, uphill most of the way. I skipped the walk down to Skaros Rock, fearing the climb back up, but the views from town were much the same. After the walk back, I picked a random restaurant very near the cable car exit: not the greatest view, but the moussaka was good. It was very hot sitting in the sun, so I ducked into an indoor bar to cool off a bit.

By about 1:30 I was certain I wouldn't last until the ~6:30pm sunset, so I headed to PK Bar on the cliff and asked if I could use my reservation credit early, which they allowed. I had a pair of drinks from their house specials section before taking the donkey trail back down to the tender.

After a short nap, I was up in time to catch the entire Cirque de Machine show — lots of staging, and I wonder if it was a semi-permanent booking for many weeks or months.

The caldera. The eruption that made this view, around 1600 BC, was one of the largest in human history: it emptied the island’s centre into the sea and buried the port town of Akrotiri under ash, preserving streets and frescoed houses much as Vesuvius would preserve Pompeii some 1,700 years later. The flooded crater left behind is roughly seven miles across. The practical side of the geography is the climb Patrick faced: Fira’s old port sits at the foot of a cliff hundreds of feet high, reached by cable car, by mule, or by the 588 steps of the Karavolades stairway — the donkey trail he rode back down.

Etna is one of the most active volcanoes in the world, but the weather wasn't great — no view of the sea or the top of the volcano, and no Etna view from Taormina either. Etna and Taormina. Etna has been erupting, on and off, for half a million years, and its eruptions have been written down for at least 2,700 of them — one of the longest documented records of any volcano, and a big reason UNESCO added it to the World Heritage List in 2013. Its summit stands about 3,350 metres up (and changes height as eruptions add and remove rock), making it the tallest active volcano in Europe. Day trips up the mountain’s south side top out around the height Patrick reached, at the Silvestri Craters — cones left by an eruption in 1892. Taormina, below, is built around its ancient theatre: first raised by the Greeks in the 3rd century BC and rebuilt by the Romans, it stages its plays against Etna and the Ionian Sea — the view the weather hid from him. Split, Croatia

Link to this day

Split. A Viator tour of Diocletian's palace — unlike the palace tour I took in May, this time I learned who Diocletian was and why the palace is in Split. It was built around AD 305 as the Roman emperor's retirement residence, and Split's old town simply grew up inside its walls: not as stunning as the story, but impressive for 1700 years old. Then 45 minutes to Trogir ('pocket Venice'): marbled alleys, a very good prosciutto sandwich at an outdoor café, and the bus back in Split at 3pm. The palace. Diocletian was the rare Roman emperor who actually retired: he abdicated on 1 May 305 and withdrew to this fortified palace on the Dalmatian coast, near his birthplace, where legend says he contentedly grew cabbages — and refused every plea to return to power. His mausoleum became Split’s cathedral in the 7th century, one of the oldest cathedral buildings still in use anywhere, and the palace cellars below his apartments survive almost intact, in part because they spent centuries filled with the town’s refuse. About 3,000 people still live inside the palace walls, which UNESCO listed in 1979. Across the water, Trogir’s whole old town — a Greek-founded island settlement — is a World Heritage Site of its own. Diocletian’s Palace

The Acropolis. It survived remarkably intact for two thousand years, serving as a church and then a mosque, until 1687, when a Venetian shell ignited the Ottoman gunpowder stored inside and blew the roof off. Oia. The blue domes have a practical history: in the 18th and 19th centuries Oia was Santorini’s maritime capital, home to shipowners whose fleets traded across the Mediterranean and beyond. The bay. The Bay of Kotor is usually called Europe’s southernmost fjord, though strictly it is a ria — a river valley drowned by the rising Adriatic — which is why it twists inland like a river rather than a glacial trench. Our Lady of the Rocks is its strangest landmark: according to legend, sailors found an icon of the Madonna on a reef here in 1452 and vowed to lay a rock on the spot after every safe voyage; over centuries the reef became an island, a church went up on it in 1632, and locals still add stones every 22 July in the fašinada procession. Link to this day

Dinner last night, since I did go out: anchovies and onions to start, cuttlefish with squid ink for the main — both flagged as typical Venetian, both ok, and nothing I'd ever seek out again. Glass and lace. Venice moved its glass furnaces to Murano in 1291 officially to protect the city from fire — but the arrangement also kept the glassmakers, and their secrets, where the Republic could watch them, and leaving the island without permission was once a serious crime. Burano’s craft was the opposite kind of work: needle lace so fine that a single piece could take months, prized at courts across Europe from the 1500s on. The painted houses have a practical legend of their own — fishermen are said to have coloured them so they could find their way home through the lagoon fog. The flood-tracking house he photographed marks November 2019, when an acqua alta of 187 centimetres, the second-highest since records began, put most of the city underwater.

Venice

The sights, briefly. St Mark’s began as the Doge’s private chapel, raised over the relics of St Mark, which Venetian merchants famously stole from Alexandria in 828. The basilica standing today dates from the 11th century, its thousands of square metres of gold mosaic added over hundreds of years; the four bronze horses above the door were loot from Constantinople in 1204 (the originals are now kept in the museum upstairs). Next door, the Doge’s Palace was the whole state in one building — residence, senate, courts and prison — and the covered Bridge of Sighs links it to the cells, named, legend says, for the prisoners’ last view of Venice. The Rialto Bridge, by the market he started in, is the oldest of only four bridges across the Grand Canal: a single stone arch completed in 1591, replacing a succession of wooden ones.
