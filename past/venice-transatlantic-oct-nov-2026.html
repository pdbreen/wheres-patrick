Seven thousand seats, then a coffee. The glass in the photos is doing exactly what it's supposed to: holding its layers. An Asiático is built, not mixed — condensed milk, brandy, Licor 43 and espresso poured over the back of a spoon so the strata stay separate under a cap of foam, cinnamon, lemon peel and coffee beans. Stirring it is the one local heresy; the layering is the drink.

It's Cartagena's signature and barely exists anywhere else. The recipe dates to the 1940s and Pedro Conesa Ortega's Bar Pedrín in El Albujón, though Cartagena fishermen were already drinking a rough ancestor — recuelo, poor-quality coffee, with brandy and milk to stay warm on night trips. It was first called the Ruso; the Cold War made that name awkward, so it became the Asiático. The squat heat-proof glass has been made by the José Díaz company since 1945, its shape borrowed from the old Santa Lucía glassworks, and the Licor 43 in it is distilled in Cartagena itself. Since 2011 the city runs a Ruta del Asiático, with bars competing on their version. Wikipedia — Asiático · Atlas Obscura — the Asiático

The theatre in the photos is the other Cartagena signature. Built between 5 and 1 BC and dedicated to Augustus's grandsons Gaius and Lucius, it held around 7,000 spectators — one of the largest theatres in Roman Hispania, its cavea carved straight out of the rock. Then it vanished: a Byzantine commercial quarter, an Islamic suburb and the medieval church of Santa María were built over it, and the city forgot it entirely until 1988, when construction work uncovered the first rows of seats. Rafael Moneo's museum (opened 2008) threads visitors under the street and beneath the old church to reach it. Spain.info — Museum of the Roman Theatre of Cartagena

The arch he walked to through the park is the other end of the story: the Arc de Triomf was Josep Vilaseca i Casanovas's gateway to the 1888 Universal Exposition, built in red Neo-Mudéjar brick as a civic welcome rather than a military triumph, its frieze reading Barcelona rep les nacions — Barcelona welcomes the nations. Four of the five lands. The Herculaneum tour was pretty good. It was buried by the same eruption of Vesuvius that destroyed Pompeii in AD 79, but where Pompeii went under falling ash, Herculaneum was hit by superheated surges that carbonized its wood — which is why so much of it survives. Very different than Pompeii. The excavation site is extremely small comparatively, but some of the frescoes and mosaics were in pretty good shape. Because of how it was buried, they also had a lot of preserved carbonized wood furniture and some wood structures like ceilings, and a boat in the museum. The skeletons were replicas — but they did find many in the boathouses, like they're shown.

Herculaneum, up close. The town was smaller and wealthier than Pompeii — a seaside resort for well-off Romans — and the eruption caught it differently: the first pyroclastic surge arrived in the night with heat so intense that it killed instantly the hundreds sheltering in the waterfront boathouses, where the remains of some 300 people were found in the 1980s. Buried under roughly 20 metres of volcanic material, the town stayed sealed until workmen digging a well struck its theatre in 1709. Much of it is still unexcavated beneath the modern town of Ercolano — including the Villa of the Papyri, whose charred scrolls are the only library to survive from antiquity. The Roman boat in the museum was found on the ancient shoreline in 1982, preserved by the same mud that buried everything else. Archaeological Park of Herculaneum

Delos and the windmills. The caldera.
