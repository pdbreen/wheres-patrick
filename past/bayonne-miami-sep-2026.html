The Bimini Road. Five visits in, he is in the right place for one of the Atlantic’s odder stories: off North Bimini lies the “Bimini Road”, a half-mile line of flat limestone blocks on the seabed, found by divers in 1968 and championed for years as a relic of Atlantis — the psychic Edgar Cayce had predicted in 1938 that part of Atlantis would turn up near Bimini in 1968 or 1969. The Wall. The abrupt depth change at his second snorkel stop is Grand Turk’s famous Wall: the reef shelf drops from snorkelling depth to thousands of feet within a short swim, because the island sits at the edge of the Turks Passage, a channel some 7,000 feet deep between the shallow banks. It makes the island a serious dive destination — and puts deep water close enough to shore for humpback whales to pass through in winter. Grand Turk also claims a place in space history: John Glenn’s Friendship 7 capsule splashed down in the Atlantic near the island in 1962, after the first American orbital flight; Glenn was brought to Grand Turk afterward, and the cruise centre keeps a splashdown exhibit telling the story.

Little Havana. Hamilton. Bermuda’s capital is younger than the colony it governs: the seat of government moved here from St George’s in 1815, and the city grew around this harbour, where the painted Front Street buildings began as merchants’ warehouses — goods unloaded at the wharf, sold at street level, the merchant living upstairs. The two wrecks. The Montana has company: she lies beside the Constellation, an American four-masted schooner wrecked on the same reef in 1943 carrying a cargo that included sacks of cement — which is exactly what he swam over. The sacks rotted away, the cement set, and the seabed there is littered with perfect bag-shaped blocks of concrete. The Montana herself was a sidewheel paddle steamer running supplies to the Confederacy when she struck in December 1863. Together they are two of the more than 300 wrecks charted around Bermuda, an island whose reefs earned it a reputation as the shipwreck capital of the Atlantic. The Dockyard where he had sushi afterwards was Britain’s answer to that geography: a fortress and naval base built up from 1809 to guard the western Atlantic.

The teardrop. The sculpture in his photo, on the Bayonne waterfront, is “To the Struggle Against World Terrorism” — a 100-foot tower split by a jagged crack, with a 40-foot stainless-steel teardrop suspended in the gap. Boleros. The lounge with the new drink menu takes its name from the bolero — the romantic Latin song form born in Santiago de Cuba, where Pepe Sánchez wrote “Tristezas”, counted the first bolero, in 1883. The shipboard version — a Latin band, a dance floor and a rum list — is its descendant, and the Captain’s Corner he went to afterwards is its opposite number: the captain and senior officers taking questions from the Crown & Anchor faithful. His four-brewery loop is a tour of Boston’s newest neighbourhood: within living memory the Seaport was parking lots and rail yards, and its bar scene is younger than some of the beers. Harpoon is the elder — founded in 1986, it was the first company in 25 years to receive a Massachusetts brewing permit, permit number 001, and its beer hall anchors the district. The whales. The spouts on this sailing were no accident of luck: the route north from Bayonne crosses the Gulf of Maine and passes Stellwagen Bank, a submerged plateau between Cape Ann and Cape Cod protected as a national marine sanctuary since 1992. Upwelling nutrients and dense schools of sand lance make it one of the world’s great whale-feeding grounds — humpbacks, fin and minke whales and Atlantic white-sided dolphins through the summer, with the endangered North Atlantic right whale among its seasonal visitors — which is why the pool deck doubles as a whale watch on this itinerary. The detour up the Hudson. Ships leaving Cape Liberty normally turn seaward — out past the Statue of Liberty and under the Verrazzano-Narrows Bridge. The left turn he got instead took the ship up the Hudson, the river that flows both ways: it is a tidal estuary for its lower 150 miles, with the tide felt as far inland as Troy, and the Lenape name for it, Muhheakantuck, means roughly “the river that flows two ways”. Lady Liberty, whom they passed at the start, has stood on her island since 1886 — a gift from France, assembled on a pedestal Americans paid for by public subscription.
