Thu · Aug 27, 2026

Dominica Favorite port

Link to this day

"Bubbles and Waterfalls" — a pretty amazing day. The Viator tour started with snorkeling at Champagne Reef, partly over an actively venting volcano: bubbles coming up from the bottom, and the water over the vents very warm. Then a gorge swim through to a waterfall you could step out into — a Pirates of the Caribbean 2 scene was filmed there. We finished at volcanic sulfur baths and rode back to the cabin smelling totally of sulfur. Lush, green, laid-back — Dominica calls itself the Nature Island of the Caribbean, and I'd go back.

The Nature Island. Dominica is the youngest and roughest of the Lesser Antilles — a volcanic island still being built, with nine volcanoes and a rainforest interior that stayed green while other islands were cleared for sugar. The geothermal show is everywhere: Champagne Reef’s vents are warm water rising through a submerged volcanic crater, and inland the same system feeds the sulfur springs he finished the day in. The island’s centrepiece is Boiling Lake, the second-largest hot lake in the world, and the whole Morne Trois Pitons area is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The gorge swim is Titou Gorge, a slot in the rock carved by a waterfall — the Pirates of the Caribbean crew filmed there for Dead Man’s Chest, choosing Dominica precisely because it still looks unbuilt.

Tue · Aug 25, 2026

St Maarten

Link to this day

St Maarten. Off the ship early for a walk into town — only two ships in (usually at least four). I walked the boardwalk end to end, then back through an empty main shopping street. Piranha Joe's for yet another shirt, then Marley's: a bucket of beer plus a few more, lunch, and good chat with staff and guests. Cheap. I definitely plan to return.

Two countries, one island. St Maarten is the smallest landmass in the world shared by two nations: the island has been split between France (Saint-Martin, the north) and the Netherlands (Sint Maarten, the south) since the Treaty of Concordia in 1648, and the border is marked by little more than a sign, a change of language and different license plates. Philipsburg, the Dutch capital, grew up on the sandbar between Great Bay — where the boardwalk runs — and the Great Salt Pond behind it, which once made the town’s fortune in salt.

CocoCay, Bahamas

Link to this day

CocoCay. Hideaway Beach — normal seat and routine, lunch on the island before heading back aboard. Forecast shows possible storms during the Rhapsody cruises out of San Juan.

Hideaway Beach. His “normal seat” sits in the newest corner of Perfect Day at CocoCay, Royal Caribbean’s private island in the Berry Islands. Hideaway Beach opened in January 2024, timed to Icon of the Seas’ debut, as the island’s first adults-only area: 18 and over, day pass required, numbers capped at about 1,800 guests a day, with a DJ-led pool and swim-up bar at its centre and quieter sand at the edges — the settled routine he describes is exactly what the place was designed to produce. The beach club. The “same chair as last time” routine was possible so soon because the place itself is new: the Royal Beach Club, Royal Caribbean’s first beach club, opened on Paradise Island, across the harbour from the Nassau cruise port, on 23 December 2025. It is an all-inclusive, day-pass destination — 17 acres with two beaches, three pools and ten bars, including the Floating Flamingo, billed as the world’s largest swim-up bar — reached by a short ferry ride from the ships, which is what makes a repeat-visit routine possible on a port day. The parks. Islands of Adventure opened in 1999 as Universal’s second Orlando park, but the resort was transformed in 2010 by the Wizarding World of Harry Potter, the land whose success rewrote what a theme-park area could be. The Hogwarts Express he rode is more than transport: added in 2014 alongside Diagon Alley in the original park, it runs between two different “stations”, shows a different ride film in each direction, and requires a park-to-park ticket because it literally crosses between the two parks. Lunch was at the CityWalk Hard Rock, which the chain bills as the largest Hard Rock Cafe in the world.

Sea day, still great weather — nearly the end of the crossing with one sea day left. Diverted to Bermuda (again!) to offload a medical emergency.

Why Bermuda, again. The diversion was barely a detour: Bermuda sits alone in the Atlantic, roughly 650 miles off the North Carolina coast, directly under the routes between the US East Coast, Florida and Europe, so ships pass within reach of it on crossings like this one. When someone aboard needs a hospital, the island’s pilot boats meet the ship offshore and land the patient for the ambulance ride to King Edward VII Memorial Hospital, Bermuda’s only acute-care hospital — a routine the island’s marine operations centre runs many times a year, which is why it keeps happening on his crossings. Thu · Jul 30, 2026

Cádiz, Spain

Link to this day

I'd liked Cádiz on a short visit with Sean long ago, so I stayed in Cádiz instead of spending the day on a bus to Seville or Jerez. Cádiz is often called the oldest continuously inhabited city in Western Europe — founded by Phoenician traders some 3,000 years ago — and it rewards staying put. I started with a Viator walking tour that meandered through the city nicely — only a few hours, leaving plenty of time for wandering, beers, and lunch before heading back to the ship to begin the crossing.

Cádiz, continued. The city he stayed in has form for crossroads moments: Columbus sailed from Cádiz on his second (1493) and fourth (1502) voyages, and three centuries later, with Napoleon occupying most of Spain, Cádiz held out behind its sea walls as the seat of the resistance government — which is where Spain’s first liberal constitution was proclaimed in 1812, nicknamed “La Pepa” because it was announced on St Joseph’s day. The old town still sits on its narrow spit of land, a city of watchtowers: merchants built more than a hundred rooftop towers so they could watch their own ships come in.

Wed · Jul 29, 2026

Málaga, Spain

Link to this day

A morning with Sean and Iza, then an afternoon with Sean after dropping Iza at work. We drove to Nerja and wandered, including the Balcony of Europe, the famous clifftop viewpoint over the Mediterranean. Lunch was up in the hill town of Frigiliana — one of Andalusia's whitewashed villages — where, after exploring the alleyways on foot all the way up, we settled at a restaurant in Plaza de las Tres Culturas. Back near the port after lunch for a smoothie and beers before the ship. The democracy debate that started in May continued.

The Balcony and the white villages. Nerja’s Balcony of Europe sits on the clifftop where a fortress once guarded this stretch of coast; the name is usually credited to King Alfonso XII, who looked out at the sea here in 1885 and is said to have declared it the balcony of a continent. A few miles away the town hides another marvel Patrick didn’t get to: the Nerja Caves, vast caverns found by five local boys in 1959, holding a stalactite-stalagmite column among the largest in the world. Frigiliana, up in the hills, is one of Andalusia’s whitewashed hill villages; its steep upper quarter, the Barribarto, keeps the street plan of its Moorish past — steps, alleys and dead ends built for feet and mules, not cars.

Tue · Jul 28, 2026

Cartagena, Spain

Link to this day

The tour started in Murcia — a guided tour plus free time to explore — then a short walking tour back in Cartagena. I left the tour near its end in Plaza de San Francisco for tapas and beer outdoors.

Cartagena’s layers. The short walking tour at the end of the day covers real depth: the city was founded by the Carthaginian general Hasdrubal around 227 BC as Qart Hadasht — “New City”, the same name as Carthage itself — and Rome took it in 209 BC. Its Roman theatre, seating some 7,000, lay hidden under later building for nearly two thousand years and was only rediscovered in 1988; excavated and opened as a museum in 2008, it is now the centrepiece of a port city that also guards its naval history in the ARQUA museum of underwater archaeology. Murcia, the morning stop, is the regional capital inland — a university city built on the Segura river’s market gardens, long nicknamed the Orchard of Europe.

Mon · Jul 27, 2026

Palma de Mallorca, Spain

Link to this day

I did a Viator tour that was kind of interesting — except the guide was upset he wasn't allowed to give the tour he wanted, and spent a lot of time on the phone arguing with the tour company while we just stood around. Very odd. We did get to Valldemossa — the mountain village where Chopin and George Sand spent a famous winter at the charterhouse — and saw a bit of Palma, but next time I'd rather walk from the ship into town and spend the day exploring, shopping, and eating. A long walk, but very doable.

Chopin’s winter. The famous winter was 1838–39: Chopin and the writer George Sand rented cells in Valldemossa’s Carthusian monastery — only recently emptied of its monks — hoping the island air would help his tuberculosis. It rained, the locals kept their distance from the unmarried couple, and Sand later wrote a waspish account, A Winter in Majorca; but Chopin completed his 24 Preludes there, and the cell now displays his Pleyel piano, shipped from Paris and famously held up for weeks on the way. Palma itself, back down the mountain, is crowned by La Seu, a Gothic cathedral whose rose window is among the largest in the world — and in the 20th century Gaudí was brought in to remodel its interior.

The long walk, explained. His plan to stroll to the World Trade Center shuttle was undone by a decision Barcelona took in 2023: the city-centre cruise terminals at the World Trade Center — five minutes’ walk from the foot of La Rambla — were closed to cruise ships, and every large ship now berths across the harbour at Moll Adossat, the Mediterranean’s busiest cruise terminal complex. The official T3 Portbus links the Adossat terminals to the Columbus Monument, which is how boarding day here is supposed to work; the full walk he ended up doing instead is the one the arrangement was designed to spare him. The fast train. Patrick’s preference for iryo has a backstory: it is a private operator, the first competitor let onto Spain’s high-speed network when the state monopoly ended — iryo’s red trains began running Madrid–Barcelona in November 2022, competing with Renfe on one of Europe’s busiest high-speed corridors, the roughly 620 kilometres between the capitals covered in as little as two and a half hours. He ended the day at La Rambla, Barcelona’s 1.2-kilometre boulevard, which follows the line of the medieval city wall and a seasonal stream bed — “rambla” is the word for just such a watercourse.

Madrid, Spain

Link to this day

An early-entry guided tour at the Prado — we were the only people in it. The Prado holds one of the world's great collections of Spanish painting, Velázquez and Goya included. It was a decent highlights tour, though I can't recall any of the details (adding this in October). I was the only one who'd also booked the palace tour, which essentially turned into a private walking tour from the Prado to the palace — the guide pointed out landmarks, lunch and dinner options, even an archaeological site behind my hotel's reception desk. My second time through the palace, and I didn't recall much from the first. Drinks in an alley outside Plaza Mayor after, then shopping on Gran Via — Primark had Patriots logo clothing on prominent display.

The Prado and the palace. The Prado opened in 1819 as a royal museum, which is why its heart is the Spanish royal collection: the world’s deepest holdings of Velázquez (Las Meninas among them) and of Goya — from sunny tapestry cartoons to the Black Paintings he made on the walls of his own house — plus El Greco, and Bosch’s Garden of Earthly Delights, a royal favourite for centuries. The palace he walked to afterward out-sizes it in a different way: Madrid’s Royal Palace is the largest functioning royal palace in Western Europe, with some 3,400 rooms, though Spain’s king lives elsewhere, at Zarzuela, and keeps this one for state ceremonies. Thu · Jul 23, 2026

Segovia, Ávila & Toledo, Spain

Link to this day

A Viator bus trip to three towns outside Madrid — Segovia, Ávila, and Toledo. Segovia's Roman aqueduct is one of the best-preserved anywhere, and Ávila is ringed by complete medieval walls. Great weather but still hot. It was a very long day, with an early start and a late return and a lot of time on the bus, but worth it. A decent burger for lunch in a plaza outside the castle in Ávila.

Three towns, three eras. Segovia’s aqueduct is the real marvel of Roman plumbing: tens of thousands of granite blocks fitted together with no mortar at all, marching 167 arches up to 28.5 metres above the Plaza del Azoguejo — and it was still carrying water into the city until 1973. Ávila’s walls survive complete: about two and a half kilometres of them, with 88 towers, guarding the highest provincial capital in Spain and the birthplace of St Teresa. Toledo, the day’s third stop, was Spain’s capital before Madrid and is still called the City of Three Cultures for the centuries when Christian, Muslim and Jewish communities lived and built side by side — and the painter El Greco made it his home. Wed · Jul 22, 2026

Madrid, Spain

Link to this day

Arrival day after the overnight business-class flight. Passport control was odd — it told me I didn't need to register, but then the gates didn't open. The Metro from the airport took me to Puerta del Sol and the NH Collection Palacio de Tepa. Very hot. Drinks and food outside in Plaza de Santa Ana — very touristy, but nice to sit outside.

Kilometre Zero. Puerta del Sol, where his metro ride ended, is the literal centre of Spain: a plaque in the pavement marks Kilometre Zero, the point from which the country’s six radial highways are measured. The clock on the old post office above it is also the one Spain watches on New Year’s Eve, when tradition is to eat twelve grapes, one with each midnight chime. The square takes its name from a 15th-century gate in the city wall, decorated with a rising sun.
